# Apollo XIII, The Board Game: Who Knew Mission Failure Could Be So Much Fun?
by Martin Gonzalvez


Ever since I was a kid, I have always wanted to be a steely-eyed missile man. I never thought I’d find a game that would let me live out my childhood dream. In my never-ending quest for hidden gems from the bargain bin, I came across Apollo XIII. Released in 2015, original MSRP $39.99, now available for $15. At rank 7,400, boardgamegeek doesn’t seem to like it. Inexpensive and unloved by most, it is a perfect candidate for my list of hidden gems.

Apollo XIII is a story-driven co-op game for 2–5 players, and the box estimates a play time of 45–75 minutes. For the first few plays, I would expect the game to end much sooner than that, mainly because of all the mission-failing and dying you’re going to be doing. This may be a family-weight game, but it’s not an easy game. Expect to replay the game with adjusted strategies a few times until you find success. But if you’re into the theme (and if you buy this game, I must assume you are), and you play the game the way it’s meant to be played, there’s a high probability that you’ll have a lot of fun along the way.

I have played this game three times so far: once solo, and twice with my daughter. We have not won yet. The furthest we have gotten is the “D” stage, and the stages go to “G”. But in our experience, as soon as we finish a game, we have the strong desire to reset and start again. At least for our family, this game has quickly become our current favorite. Even as I write this, I’m wondering why I’m spending time writing this review when I could be starting up another Apollo XIII mission and trying out a different strategy, in the hopes of having a better outcome.

Why do we like it so much? First of all, we love the delicious tension that the game manages to create through its effective design.


Mission Control Board

Opening the box, the first component that caught my eye was the game board designed to look like NASA Mission Control, where the ground flight crew worked to monitor and the save the lives of the three Apollo XIII astronauts. The top half has Stress level and Body Health status monitors for Mission Commander Jim Lovell, LEM Pilot Fred Haise, and CSM Pilot Jack Swigert. To the right of those are status monitors for the Rocket, and Mission Control itself. Underneath those are Flight and Energy statuses, which if you manage to build up, you can later spend to lessen or eliminate
negative effects. Finally, the lower third of the board is the overall Mission Status meter. This will increase each time an astronaut stress/health meter, rocket or mission control meter rises over a certain critical level. You use the included white wooden cubes to keep track of each of the various statuses.


Hourglass Timer

If the Mission Status reaches orange, you must use the included sand hourglass timer to give yourselves just one minute of real time to make a decision of what action to take on your turn. If the Mission Status goes into the red area, you will now have just 30 seconds to decide on actions or forfeit! Finally, if your turn ends with the Mission Status in the Mission Failed area on the lower right of the board, then the astronauts have lost their lives, and Apollo XIII goes hurtling out of control, lost in the depths of space. Personally, my daughter and I really loved the unique mechanic of using the timer. It really helped to give us an idea of what it feels like to make critical decisions under time pressure, with little room for error. Just as it was for the three astronauts, and for Mission Control. This timed element led to a lot of yelling and frantic looking through cards, and potentially hasty decisions. Most of all, it was just great fun. This is part of what I meant earlier when I said that if you play this game the way it’s meant to be played, you can have great fun with it.



Story Cards

The game has seven Story Event cards that you arrange in an oval that stretches from the included cardboard Earth token, to the cardboard Moon token.

By the way, I read a review that criticized the included Earth and Moon tokens as essentially useless. While they serve no mechanical or functional purpose, in my opinion they do serve a great thematic purpose. Personally, I felt my pulse quicken with excitement each time I arrange those cardboard pieces on the table. So I would submit that people who consider these useless pieces of cardboard are probably not into the theme of this game. Which I would then question, why did you buy this game in the first place? The name and the box art make it very clear what this game is going to be all about.

In any event, these Story Event cards, marked “A” through “G”, correspond to seven pivotal events in the story of the Apollo XIII mission. Underneath each Story Event card, you set up a deck of Story Cards marked with the same letter as the respective Story Event card above it. These Story Decks provide more details and fascinating trivia around that particular phase in the Apollo XIII mission. For example, there’s a card that relates the famous story of original Command Module pilot Ken Mattingly being replaced at the last minute by backup CM pilot Jack Swigert, due to fears that Mattingly had been exposed to German Measles. This last minute replacement caused conflict and stress to the other two crew members, stresses which are reflected in the game’s mechanics. The game provides some variability in that each Story Deck is shuffled to relate certain events in a random order, within that phase of the Story.

But within each Story Deck is a pivotal Event card. Once that pivotal event card is drawn, the effects of that event must be resolved, and now players draw Story cards from the next Story Event deck. And so on, from pivotal Event transitioning to next Story Deck, until you successfully bring our brave astronauts home (note: I have not achieved this yet).

One criticism I have read leveled is that “the game is on rails,” or feels like “the game is playing you,” rather than you playing the game (whatever that means). To those critiques, I reply — this is a story-driven game! Of course it’s on rails! This game is not designed as a modular choose-your-own-adventure, with vignettes that can work when revealed at any point in the story. If that’s what you’re looking for, play Eldritch Horror instead — an excellent game of mini-stories, by the way.

Apollo XIII is a game that, at least initially, is designed to tell the whole story of this fateful space mission in order, from beginning to end. Therefore, it wouldn’t make sense for the landing to come before the takeoff, or for the solution to a crisis event to come before the crisis itself. Another complaint I read had to do with certain cards having instructions to put it back into the deck if another event card had not yet been drawn. Again, I consider this part of the game’s (initially) linear-story driven mechanic. At least for the first few games, the rules recommend that you play the story in order, and true to the actual events. Once you’ve mastered those, the game comes with variant Event cards that presumably make the game more challenging (I haven’t tried them yet).

Player Cards

Another major element of this game is the Player Cards. You shuffle the Player Card deck, then each player draws a starting hand of seven cards. You have a general hand limit of ten cards, although some cards grant a temporary bonus to your hand limit. The Player Cards have specific abilities printed on them that are one way to counteract the negative effects brought on by the Story Cards — although crucially, not the only way (more on this later). Many Player Cards are marked with a letter, indicating that the card’s positive ability can only be played if you are currently within the part of the story that matches that letter. So, for example, if the crew’s stress level rises because they are on national TV (Story Deck “B”), you could play a Player Card marked “B” that features a crew member telling jokes to get them all to relax, thus lowering their stress level.

I really like card games that feature multi-use cards (*cough* 51st State *cough*), and this game has that feature in spades. Playing a Player Card directly for its printed ability is actually very rare, because it requires that you have a card in your hand with a letter that matches the letter of the current Story Card. If you limit your card play to hanging on to your Player Cards until the right moment in the story, you will lose, and lose badly. Fortunately, this game provides a range of other opportunities to make your Player Cards useful:

1. Play your card for its printed ability (very rare)

2. Place your card to the left of the game board. Limited to cards with a letter matching the current Story Card. Accumulating two of these will allow you to lower one status meter by two places, or two status meters by one step (except for the main Mission Status).

3. Place your card to the right of the game board. Must be a card whose letter does *not* match the current Story Card, or a card with no letter at all. Accumulating three of these will allow you to lower one status meter by two steps, or two status meters by one step (except for the main Mission Status).

4. Discard any Player Card to gain an extra action on your turn. The extra action must be different from any earlier action already taken on your turn.

One thing we are learning is that hoarding Player Cards is a surefire way to early disaster. You must maximize the use of your available Player Cards on each turn, yet also decide which cards to hang on to as they might turn out to be useful for later in the game. It’s elements like these that make this game more strategically complex and satisfying than might seem apparent on first, second or even third play. So use your cards, use them wisely, and don’t hang on to them for too long.

NASA Tokens

One final game element is the NASA tokens. In the co-op version of this game, only one side of the tokens is used. The game rules provide a competitive variant in which the tokens are used to determine which player scored the most points in the game (we haven’t tried this variant yet). If you manage to lower any status below the critical level during your turn, you earn a NASA token. The benefit of the NASA token is that you may spend it to take an action during another player’s turn. For example, if you have a card that can be combined with another player’s card to have a greater positive effect, this would be an ideal use of the NASA token. Another use would be to help out your teammate by playing a card from your hand that can positively affect the current Story, in case they lack a relevant card in their hand.

Game Flow and Available Actions

The game flow is easy to learn, and easy to teach. Included in the box are five handy gameplay summary and reference cards. On each player’s turn, you:

1. Draw a Story Card.
2. Resolve any negative status updates.
3. Draw a Player Card, add it to your hand.
4. Take one of the available Actions:
4a. Play a Player Card (must match current Story letter)
4b. Move one status cube.
4c. Place a Player Card to the left of the game board (must match current Story letter)
4d. Place a Player Card to the right of the game board (must not match current Story letter, or have no letter)
4e. Draw another Player Card.

These are the basics of a player turn in Apollo XIII. However, what we are discovering is that you must figure out ways to maximize the number of extra actions you take on your turn. As mentioned earlier, you can discard a Player Card to take one extra action, one that is different from any previous action you took. So if earlier you placed a Player Card beside the board, your extra action must consist of something different, like pushing a cube, drawing another Story Card, or playing a card. You must also make sure to acquire and make good use of NASA tokens, as playing these to take actions when it’s not your turn is critical to success. This is a true cooperative game in that players must communicate and coordinate their available cards to achieve success. Beneath the seemingly simple menu of available actions lie more complex strategies that wait for you to discover and employ.

Components

The production value on this game is a little uneven. On the positive side, the cards themselves have a substantial, premium, linen feel to them. They feel thicker and take more effort to shuffle than standard playing cards, to my hands. The board feels adequately solid, the cardboard tokens feel solid and thick enough to be unlikely to warp. The white wooden cubes are…just that, white wooden cubes. They get the job done.

The NASA Apollo XIII logo on the back of the Player Cards is bright and adds to the flavor.

On the negative side, the Story and Player cards are not designed in an appealing way, in my opinion. They are text-heavy, without good use of white space or creative typography. The card art uses a mix of hand-drawn line art, and NASA archival photographs. The archive photography is very cool and adds to the flavor, but at the same time I feel that it could have been enhanced by adding color and extra glitz to the presentation. That said, these are relatively minor quibbles that do not detract greatly from the fun gameplay.

Summing Up

Apollo XIII is a great family-weight game that can provide lots of addictive fun, if you’re into the theme and you play it correctly. Accept that the game is designed to tell a story. Read and savor the flavor text on each Story and Player card. Collaborate with each other the way the astronauts needed to. Use your wits and ingenuity to maximize the number and types of actions you take on your turn, they way the astronauts had to use their wits and ingenuity to figure out how to solve seemingly hopeless problems and get home. Use the NASA tokens at crucial points to take extra actions outside of your turn. Look for opportunities to pair your cards up with your teammates’. Use the timer to add tension and fun to your decision-making when the Mission Status is in the orange or red areas. If you play this game the way it’s meant to be played, you’re sure to enjoy it. Even if you fail a bunch of missions on the way to success.

PROS:

- Easy to learn, easy to teach, card driven game mechanics with low luck factor
- True cooperative gameplay that will lead to lots of fun yelling and cool tense moments
- Challenging; will keep you coming back for more to devise and uncover different strategies for success
- I’m no rocket scientist, but the theme is great (lol)
- Inexpensive; lots of game bang for not a lot of bucks

CONS:

- Component quality is a little bit underwhelming
- Cards are text-heavy, with a sometimes uninspiring mix of (admittedly still cool) archival pictures and lackluster line art
- (but then again, you’re getting a lot of game for a great price, so quit your whining!)

Verdict, after three plays (with more to come): Apollo XIII is a certified hidden gem!
