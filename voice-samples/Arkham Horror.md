# How I learned to stop worrying and love Arkham Horror: The Card Game
Martin Gonzalvez


Role-playing, Deck Building, Co-op or Solo Play

Investigators blasting away at other-worldly creatures in the 1920’s — on paper, the perfect game for me.
I’ve loved H. P. Lovecraft and the Cthulhu mythos since my teens (although “loved” might be the wrong characterization. More like “been fascinated by” or “found myself unhealthily obsessed with”). Playing Chaosium’s classic Call of Cthulhu campaign, Masks of Nyarlathotep with my college gaming group, remains a beloved gaming memory. One that I’ve not been able to recapture since.

I realize that nowadays the Cthulhu theme has been (over)done so many times in so many different games, that many roll their eyes and shake their heads when they see yet another dark, forbidding, Lovecraftian offering. But for me, it’s been a constant thread in the fabric of my life.

So when I bought my copy of Fantasy Flight’s Arkham Horror: The Card Game (AH:TCG) a few months ago, I was primed to absolutely love it. The hype for this game was off the charts. The promise of a story-driven card game with the feel of a pen-and-paper RPG, character customization and growth, earning of experience, permanent changes over the course of a campaign, playable by 1–4 players, all set in a 1920’s Cthulhu universe, and without need of a GameMaster — this was a heady brew indeed.

Then I played it.

Then I played it a second time.

Things did not go well.

Here’s how I described my experience at the time:

I tried, I really tried my darnedest to like it. But each time I play, I just don’t have any fun. Where do I start with my issues with this game? First, setup is a pain. Checking card numbers against the list to build the characters’ starting decks is annoying and time-consuming. Second, the *three* included rule books overlap with duplicate information, which is confusing, and finding information quickly can be an exercise in frustration. Which exacerbates my third issue, that gameplay frequently grinds to a halt while I am obliged to search through those rule books, to decide how to proceed in a given situation. Fourth, the Chaos tokens don’t feel as exciting as dice to modify and resolve skill tests. I have yet to feel the same kind of anticipation, excitement or emotional involvement from pulling out a Chaos token, as I do rolling dice. Speaking of skill tests, that’s my fifth issue; in AH:TCG, *everything* is a skill test. Enter a room? Skill test. Investigate? Skill test. Collect the clue? Skill test. Activate an ability? Skill test. In this game, you’d better like skill tests, because you’ll be doing a metric crapload of them.

I’ll add another stumbling block to enjoyment: the game’s heavy use of iconography. Frequently, we had to stop play to pore through the Reference Guide to decode the meaning behind the various icons printed on certain cards: the squiggly lightning bolt (free triggered ability), the arrow curving back upon itself (reaction-triggered ability), or the right-facing arrow (action-triggered ability, which uses up one of your three available actions per round). Let’s not even mention the six different possible icons for Chaos token effects, or the four investigator skill icons. Learning to play AH:TCG demands that new players familiarize themselves with whole new pictographic language.

I was disappointed and discouraged. Last month I even told my son that he could take the box home, to see if perhaps he and his friends in LA would get more use and enjoyment out of it. He cared so little that he forgot to take the box.

Yesterday, I decided to give the game one last chance. I sat down to play it solo. I brought reinforcements: the Esoteric Order of Gamers Rules Summary and Reference, and Rahdo Runs Through Arkham Horror: The Card Game. I devoted nearly three hours hours to the effort. I doggedly pushed through all of the annoyances mentioned above. I gulped down two large cups of coffee. I stuck with it.

And then, this happened:

That sweet, sweet moment when Agent Banks empties his .45 automatic into the infernal creature with one hand, then finishes it off with the machete in the other.
That one glorious moment when, after all of my struggling with rules and icons and rule books and mechanics, it all suddenly melted away, and I got swept up in the sheer fun and drama of the game. My character, FBI Agent Roland Banks, confronted the infernal final creature with just a .45 automatic in one hand, and a machete in the other. Aided by a string of fortuitous Chaos token draws, Banks emptied the clip into the creature; then when was out of ammo, he successfully hacked the monster to bits to achieve the best possible resolution to the scenario. It just felt cool. The genuine adrenaline rush of triumph I felt in that moment is the reason why we play games, board or otherwise. The fact that AH:TCG was capable of delivering such a moment, even in solo play, despite the (still valid) criticisms mentioned earlier, is a testament to the brilliance of Fantasy Flight’s game design.

At heart, AH:TCG is a modern evolution of Fighting Fantasy-style role-playing game books. In the box is a Campaign Guide that describes the included “Night of the Zealot” campaign. Through your actions, choices and skill test results, you determine which of the various scenario resolutions and plot twists you will experience. Once you’ve played your way through the pack-in campaign, you can continue your adventure by selecting from nearly two dozen expansions that Fantasy Flight has already put out since the game’s 2016 release, or print and play some excellent fan-made scenarios.

Unlike the Fighting Fantasy game books, which used dice and randomized character attributes to add a touch of role-playing and personalization, AH:TCG has cards. Lots of cards. Cards that provide a hitherto unmatched degree of customization and potential for making the character and the adventure your own. By the way, the cards look beautiful, and are absolutely bursting with theme and flavor. Fantasy Flight does not disappoint with the art quality on these cards.

First, the Investigator Cards. Five investigators are included in the core game box, although only two are playable at the same time (more on this later). Your investigator card notes your base scores in Willpower, Intellect, Combat and Agility, your investigator’s backstory, as well as special abilities that trigger when certain game conditions are met.
Each investigator starts the game with a deck of Asset, Event, and Ally cards, known collectively as the Investigator Deck. These cards can be used two ways: either played (for a cost) as a persistent buff to your abilities (such as Agent Banks’ .45 automatic, that adds a +1 bonus to combat and to damage), or used and discarded as a one-time modifier to a skill test. This system puts a lot of power into the players’ hands to influence skill tests in their favor, and minimizes random failures. Even better, these cards are thematically consistent with your investigator’s persona and backstory. For example, Agent Banks never goes anywhere without his trusty .45 automatic, while street urchin Wendy Adams has the precious necklace with the strange eye-in-star design that her mother gave her. Players can customize their investigator decks to their liking, following rules in the How To Play guide. You might want to beef up your investigation skills, upgrade or downgrade your combat skills, or make sure that particular items are included. In true Lovecraftian fashion, buried within each Investigator Deck is a weakness card, whose negative effects will need to be suffered and resolved by your investigator at some point in the campaign. This level of character customization really elevates the game experience. However, novice players will want to stick to the recommended starter decks for each investigator, outlined in the Campaign Guide.
Each scenario is comprised of a set of Location Cards. Investigators will work to uncover and collect clues in these locations that will advance the story in their favor. Treacherous events and enemies from the Encounter Deck (see below) will block our heroes’ progress.
There’s the Act Deck and Agenda Deck, narrative devices which tell two sides of the story — a particularly brilliant piece of balanced game design, in my opinion. The Act Deck advances if the heroes are succeeding at their assigned tasks: searching locations, collecting clues, dispatching baddies, and solving the mystery. The Agenda Deck advances if the villains succeed at stalling/stopping the heroes from achieving their objectives. In general, players are motivated to advance the Act Deck, but things will start getting tense and interesting if bad stuff happens, and the Agenda Deck advances. This creates a delicious sense of tension and dread that increases the longer you play — another time-honored element of the best Call of Cthulhu campaigns.
Finally, there’s the Encounter Deck, filled with enemies and treachery to spoil our heroes’ day. The Campaign Guide comes with instructions for building an Encounter Deck that is thematically consistent with the current scenario. This ensures that in-game events make logical sense based on the scenario, and that role-playing integrity is maintained.

Mythos Phase: Add a Doom Token to the current Agenda card. If the Doom Limit is reached, advance to the next Agenda card (bad stuff happens). Then each player draws a card from the Encounter Deck, potentially drawing a Treachery or Enemy that must be confronted.

Investigation Phase: Investigators take turns performing three actions from a menu of possibilities, including: drawing a card, gaining a resource (used to purchase cards to put in play), investigating the current location for clues, playing a card, engaging, fighting or evading an enemy, or moving to another location.

Enemy Phase: Enemies engage or attack.

Upkeep Phase: Investigators manage the cards in their hand and in play, and draw one new card and one additional resource.

A word about the included Chaos tokens: AH:TCG uses these instead of dice to modify chances of success or failure at skill tests. I stand by my earlier complaint that drawing random tokens from a bag (or in my case, a mug) doesn’t feel as satisfying as rolling dice. However, the Chaos token system is like customizable dice. Negative modifiers can be added to make the game harder, or removed to make the game easier. So while I maintain that dice are more emotionally satisfying, my intellect will allow that Chaos tokens are a clever system, and many find them a nice change of pace from dice.

AH:TCG is an ambitious game, and for the most part, it succeeds. However, as I mentioned earlier, it’s not flawless by any means. The super-involved deck construction, the intentionally open-ended rules spread out across multiple books, and the cryptic iconography are all barriers to entry for newbies. Fantasy Flight could have done a much better job with onboarding new players. They could have included a brief tutorial scenario to provide a guided step-by-step playthrough, to gradually familiarize players with the game flow, and show them how to gracefully resolve potential pain points.

The game’s business model is another point for or against this game, depending on your preference and available disposable income. AH:TCG is one of a growing line of Living Card Games (LCG), which are intended as an alternative to the Collectible Card Game (CCG) model of games such as Magic: The Gathering. Nonetheless, if you want to play beyond the campaign included in the core box, Fantasy Flight wants you to buy expansions, at $15-$30 a pop, that offer new campaigns, different locations, and more Investigator Cards to develop your character. Your investment in this game can quickly add up to hundreds of dollars. It’s up to you to decide if the experience is worth the expense. Personally, I’m still working my way through the first campaign, so I’ll make that decision down the road.

In fact, even though the core box features five investigators, it comes with just enough investigator cards to build only two starter decks. You’ll need to buy a second core set (at $40 each) if you want to have enough cards to outfit and play a party of 3–4 investigators.

Summing up, AH:TCG comes tantalizingly close to achieving its lofty goal of a story-driven, fully customizable RPG in card game form. The game has flaws which can turn off novice players: steep learning curve, confusing rules, cryptic icons, intricate deck construction, and an expensive business model. If you persevere past these issues, you can have a deeply satisfying cooperative or solo game experience in a 1920’s Lovecraftian world.

Pros:

Included investigator cards offer vast options to customize your investigator and make them your own.
Brilliant Act/Agenda Deck game design rewards good gameplay, punishes poor gameplay (or bad luck), and provides a sense of drama and impending doom.
Excellent cooperative gameplay, but also works well as a solo experience.
Strong thematic elements really bring the 1920’s Lovecraftian setting and situations to vivid life.
Campaign play, earned experience, and acquired assets/allies provide a sense of continuity and accomplishment over multiple sessions — truly, an RPG without a GameMaster.
Cons:

New players can expect a high learning curve with long periods of confusion, and frequent interruptions of play to look up rules.
Rule books are not as clear as they could be, largely due to the need for open-ended support for many possible campaign scenarios.
Game icons can be intimidating to new players.
Core box contains only enough cards for two players — purchasing another box is required to support 3–4 players at the same time.
Extra purchases are required for more campaigns and further character development.
