# Starship Merchants: It’s Hard Out Here in the Belt
by Martin Gonzalvez


I purchased Starship Merchants because the 1920’s pulp sci-fi, Tom Swift-y art style caught my eye. More significantly for my hurting wallet, it was on sale for just under ten bucks. Improbably, this inexpensive, humble, unassuming little space game has quickly become a family favorite. My daughter will choose to play this over games that cost 4–5 times as much. My son, who usually only plays Pandemic, was charmed by this game as well.

Starship Merchants is a space-faring economic simulation game for 2–4 players. You are the CEO of a fledgling space mining and transport company. You start out with 15 million credits (sounds like a lot, but trust me, it’s not), a couple of ore deposits, and not much else. You must use your wits, ingenuity and business acumen to grow your company into a multi-million credit space cargo fleet. Along the way, you’ll have to fend off cutthroat competitors who won’t think twice about stealing your upgrades, mines, ore deposits, and job opportunities out from under you, if given half the chance.

At a high level, the game is pretty straightforward to outline. You have to buy ships, upgrade them, explore mines for ore deposits, fill your cargo holds, take on missions to various destinations, and make a profit by selling ore and completing missions each time around the board. First player to 100 million credits can trigger the end game. Or if you think you need a few more runs in the Belt to secure victory, you can play on until 150 million credits, after which the final turn must be declared. Hey, all good things have to end sometime. The player with the most credits at the end of the game, wins.

The game board has four areas, which correspond to four distinct phases of play: the Shipyard, the Market, The Belt, and Space Dock.

The Shipyard

Each business cycle starts here. The main actions in The Shipyard are buying ships to add to your fleet, and paying off your ship loans, if any (more on this in a bit). Eventually you will have the means and technological advancement to buy a fancy Mark III or Mark IV model, with oodles of energy and ample cargo space. But at the start of the game, all you can afford is a humble Mark I. You have a choice of a Scout (slightly cheaper), or a Tug (slightly more expensive). Scouts have more energy but less cargo capacity — more well-suited to completing missions than hauling ore deposits. Tugs have less energy but more cargo space; these workhorses will form the backbone of your less glamorous, but presumably profitable, mining operation.

You also choose how to acquire your ships: do you purchase them outright, or do you lease them? Paying full price means you pay less for your ships in the long run, but you’ll have fewer credits in the short term to buy potentially game-changing ship upgrades. Whereas, taking out a loan will give you more credits-on-hand to buy upgrades, but you’ll end up paying 1.5 times the full price of each ship, which can really add up if you suddenly have to pay off loans on multiple ships. Running up and paying off massive loans on each of my ships was how I lost (badly) to my daughter (188 million credits) and son (166 million credits) in our last game (I ended up with just 79 million credits).

Ships going obsolete is another strategic element, one that this game reportedly shares with far more complex 18XX games (I haven’t played one of those yet, so I wouldn’t know). Once a player buys a Mark III ship, everyone’s Mark I ships become obsolete. Same thing when someone buys the first Mark IV, the Mark II’s go obsolete. Obsolete ships can finish any mining hauls or mission runs they are currently on, but once they get to the dock, they are decommissioned and out of service. Deciding the most advantageous moment to cause your opponents’ ships to become obsolete can be a pivotal strategic choice in this game.

The Market

Once you’re done with Shipyard actions, your next stop is The Market. Here, you can purchase components to upgrade your ships — from widgets that grant more energy or cargo capacity, to widgets that make it easier and cheaper to explore mines and acquire ore deposits. You might also have the chance to hire pilots who grant bonuses for delivering certain types of cargo, bonuses for delivering no cargo, discounts for purchasing upgrades, discounts for exploring mines, or similar advantages. The upgrades and pilots you equip can greatly determine your specific strategy, so make sure to read and understand the effects of each and play accordingly.

I should mention here that the descriptions of some ship upgrades and pilot effects can be confusing, as they are not very clearly explained in the included rule book. Fortunately, the designers provided an FAQ that really helps to clarify these items.

In the Market, you also have the option of purchasing a Refinery. Refineries provide a bonus from each ore deposit of a certain type that you deliver. So if you have explored and found a lot of Ice-9 ore, for example, you would do well to purchase an Ice-9 Refinery, which will grant you an extra million credits for each Ice-9 deposit you deliver.

The final action you can perform in The Market is to claim a mine. Explored mines are added in your play area, which is called Local Space. Mines start out unclaimed, so even if they are in your local space, opponents can snatch them from your local space if they have enough energy to do so. To prevent other players from filching the mines in your local space, you may spend six million credits to stake your claim on that mine. Once a mine has been claimed, no other player can take it away from you. Ore from claimed mines are also worth more when your ships unload them at the Space Dock.

The Belt

The Belt is where most of the action takes place. Here is where you send your starships to do runs. You may load ore deposits from local space, or known space (the circular area at the center of the board) into your ships’ cargo holds. You may spend energy to explore for more mines from which to pick up new ore deposits. If you have enough energy, you can steal ore deposits from your opponents’ local space. And you can choose to accept a mission to travel to various destinations, if the price is right and it works in your favor. Each of your ships may complete one run per turn, so if you have multiple ships in your fleet, you will want to spend multiple turns in The Belt, until all of your ships have completed their runs.

Space Dock

Once all of your ships have completed their runs in The Belt, it’s time to head to Space Dock. Here, you will unload your cargo and get handsomely rewarded for each ore deposit you deliver, and each mission to a destination that you have successfully completed. Once you tally up all of your profits, including any bonuses and multipliers provided by destinations, pilots and refineries, you draw the funds from the bank, then head off to the Shipyard, where your next business cycle will commence.

Choices

At heart, Starship Merchants is a medium-weight economic engine-building game, with a bit of pick-up-and-deliver thrown in for flavor. Winning the game comes down to making a series of choices about how to spend your hard-earned credits, that should (ideally) enhance your earning efficiency and make you even more credits. The game provides many interesting investment choices. Starting in the Shipyard: should I buy ships outright, or lease? I’ve played both ways — won the game without any loans, and lost the game with loans, badly, for what that’s worth. Also, should I buy a ship every time I stop at the Shipyard, or should I hold off until the next turn?

At the Market, available upgrade choices are randomly determined by the two Market cards that are face up when you arrive. You can swap out one market card for free once per turn, but after that you either purchase or pass. Upgrades can add significant capabilities to your ships that can shape your play choices. For example, I once hired a pilot who gave me a discount on the amount of energy required to steal ore deposits from another player’s local space. You can bet that I was stealing ore from my kids left and right in that game! Another choice is the purchase of a Refinery. In our first two-player game, I eventually ended up buying Refineries for all three types of ore, but in a three- or four-player game, it will be harder to buy them all.

In the Belt, each ship has a limited amount of energy and cargo space, and these are the commodities that you will find yourself spending in this phase of the game. It costs one energy to load one ore deposit in a ship’s cargo hold, two energy to explore for more mines, three energy to steal an ore deposit from another player’s local space. Deciding how to spend your limited energy reserves provides a deliciously crunchy decision space. Mission cards provide another option — each mission costs from one to five energy, with variable rewards. Judicious budgeting and expenditure of energy can earn you big bucks once you get to Space Dock.

As mentioned earlier, once any player reaches 100 million credits, they have the option to declare the Final Run. Deciding the right moment to declare the Final Run is potentially the make-or-break choice in the game. For example, in our last game, my son was the first player to hit the 100 million credit milestone, but looking at his sister’s current cash hoard and heavily-laden cargo ships, he realized that she would defeat him once she arrived at Space Dock and got paid. He decided to wait and make a few final runs in the Belt before declaring the Final Run. In the end it didn’t matter, because his sister still ended up besting him. But the Final Run choice could be they key to victory in most games.

Flaws, not Dealbreakers

Starship Merchants has two rather pronounced issues. The first is that in our experience, it plays significantly longer than the 90 minutes printed on the box. Each time our family has played, we have gone around 2.5 hours or more. Some might feel that the game overstays its welcome, but in our experience, games have remained tense and enjoyable all the way to the end, despite the longer than expected playing time.

The second issue is confusing text on certain upgrade and pilot cards and items that the rule book does a poor job of clarifying, if at all. The worst example in my onion is the Ore Scanner, whose corrected action explanation is “Explore with 1 redraw to find the named ore type.” Even this corrected version is hopelessly convoluted, and the rule book does not provide an explanation of each upgrade card or pilot effect. This was provided in a separate document, not included in the box. Once you print out or consult the Card Clarification and include it in the box, the issue is greatly alleviated.

Bottom line

Starship Merchants is a solid business simulation with a unique classic space theme. If you’re willing to look beyond its minor flaws, you can have a lot of fun with this game, racing each other to build the most profitable interstellar shipping fleet.

PROS:

- Mid-weight economic efficiency engine builder with fun, easy-to-teach gameplay
- A wide variety of choices means many different paths to victory
- Inexpensive; lots of gaming bang for not a lot of bucks
- Unique, eye-catching pulp sci-fi art style and theme

CONS:

Plays significantly longer than 90 minutes
- Certain ship upgrade cards effects are confusing
- Included rule book is vague in some spots, and fails to explain key elements
- Extra FAQ required to address rule book and card confusion
Note: After I published this review on BGG, I got a response from Tom Lehmann, who co-designed this lovely, hidden gem of a game.
