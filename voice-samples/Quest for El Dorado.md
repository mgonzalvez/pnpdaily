# Game Review: The Quest for El Dorado


Deck Builder, Racing, Board/Hex Movement, Area Control

This hit our family’s table tonight, and we had so much fun that as soon as we finished the first play, we instantly turned around and played again (my daughter, 21, won both times). It’s a deck builder, it’s a board game, and it’s a frantic, strategic race between intrepid explorers to reach El Dorado, the fabled city of gold. There’s a reason why this game was runner-up for 2017 German Game of the Year — because it’s a sheer blast to play.

When setting this game up, the first components that catch the eye are several cardboard hex grids that interlock to form a long, twisting, serpentine game board that represents the arduous path to El Dorado. Each grid hex/space is a type of terrain — jungle, water, villages, barren rock, or base camps — that explorers may traverse on their path to El Dorado — for a price. The instructions provide many alternative board configurations that increase the challenge level and replayability. On the other hand, the expansive game board requires a lot of table real estate, which could limit playability in smaller spaces.

Players play cards that represent expedition team members, vehicles, special items, and money, to facilitate their travel across the spaces on the board. At first, your cards/resources are limited, sufficient only to get started on the journey. You will quickly need to spend money to acquire upgraded cards to overcome the many obstacles in your chosen path. You will also want to purge unneeded cards from your deck (at base camps) to ensure that your deck is properly optimized for the path before you. The explorer whose deck is heavy with jungle crossing cards is going to get stuck and fall behind, until they can hire a boat and a sailor to cross a river. By the way, the card art is really well done, dripping with thematic flavor.

Cards can be used either for movement, or to purchase upgrades. This leads to many points where players must decide which option is the best use for the cards in their hand. That choice is modified by their current position on the board, and what types of terrain lie ahead in their path toward the golden city. This ingenious interlocking web of factors that players must consider comprises what makes this game strategic and fun, yet the brisk game flow never gets bogged down by analysis paralysis.

My one minor complaint about the cards is that they are small, less than half the size of a standard playing card. This makes card text hard to read, and makes the cards trickier to sleeve. That said, with the board taking up so much table space, and with the sheer number of different cards available to purchase, it’s understandable why the designers had to make the cards smaller.

There’s ample opportunity for player interaction and good-natured one-upmanship. Players compete for the cards they need to get ahead — which opens up the possibility that other players could steal the cards/resources you need and slow you down. And vice versa. Players also jockey for advantageous positions on the board, since no two players can occupy the same space. This means players can strategically block opponents’ path forward, which introduces a slight area control element as well.

Game flow is brisk, mechanics are easy to teach, components are colorful and attractive, especially the card art . The addition of a game board with different terrain types adds a nice twist to the usual deck builder game mechanic. Rather than constantly angling for the most expensive cards you can afford, you are forced to purchase cards based on the particular terrain types in your way. The racing mechanic adds a nice touch of urgency and excitement to the whole affair.

I really like how this game deftly blends many different game mechanics — deck builder, racing, board/hex movement, and area control — into a cohesive whole. It’s a family-weight game with variant rules and board configurations that can increase the challenge level for more advanced gamers.

Pros:

Seamless blend of deck builder, racing game, and board/hex movement, with a touch of area control
Colorful and attractive game board and components
Easy to learn and teach
Includes tougher game variants and more challenging game board configurations to up the difficulty level
Cons:

Expansive game board requires a lot of table space
Cards are smaller than average, making card text hard to read, and making cards a challenge to sleeve
