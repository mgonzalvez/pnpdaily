# Weekly Briefing: The Current BGG PnP Contest Run Is Stacked

*By Martin Gonzalvez | March 11, 2026*

---

If you have been sitting around waiting for the right BoardGameGeek print and play contest to show up and magically fit your exact designer temperament, I have good news: that moment is now. The current lineup is stacked. Not in the fake "there is something for everyone" marketing sense. I mean there are actually several live contests right now with very different constraints, which means there is a decent chance one of them lines up with the way you already like to design.

And that matters, because most of us do not have one generic contest game sitting in a folder, ready to be repainted and shoved into whatever challenge happens to be open this month.

## The one that is hardest to hide from

The **2026 9-Card Nanogame Print and Play Design Contest** is exactly the kind of brief that strips away excuses. You get one sheet of nine poker-size cards, as many rules pages as you need, and up to 24 generic components total, or none at all. Contest ends **May 31, 2026**.

I like this one because the constraint is brutally clear. Nine cards is not "I just need a little more content." Nine cards is not "once I finish the second module." Nine cards is nine cards. You either have a design that fits the box or you do not.

That does not make it easy. It makes it honest.

## If you think in direct conflict

The **2026 Two-Player Print and Play Game Design Contest** has a refreshingly straightforward premise: make an inexpensive and innovative game specifically for two players. Not "supports two." Not "technically works at two if both players are patient." Specifically for two. It also runs through **May 31, 2026**.

That is a good brief, because two-player design has its own demands. Tempo matters more. Interaction matters more. Dead turns feel worse. Bluffing, pressure, timing windows, and positional play all have more room to breathe when the design is not trying to also accommodate three or four other people at the table.

## If you like a strong hook up front

The **2026 Deck Hand Games Design Contest** asks you to make an 18-card game that works in conjunction with a standard deck of playing cards. Deadline is **June 26, 2026**.

That is already a pitch.

Some contest prompts are broad enough that your game idea still needs a sentence to become interesting. This one hands you the sentence. The trick, of course, is making sure the standard deck is actually doing meaningful work in the design. If the deck feels bolted on, players will smell that immediately.

## If you have ever muttered "this game really needs a better solo mode"

The **2026 Solomode Contest** is built around designing a solo mode for a game that either does not have one, or has one you think is lacking. Deadline is **June 30, 2026**.

I like this contest because it gives designers permission to do a kind of work that is genuinely useful, but sometimes treated like it is less respectable than building something from zero. Nonsense. A good solo mode is design work. Hard design work, usually. There is a lot of bad solo grafting out there, and anything that encourages people to do it properly is fine by me.

## Two more contests, two very different muscles

The **2026 Children & Family Game Design Contest** is aimed at games families can play with children nine and under, or games children can play together. Voting closes **May 15, 2026**.

Designing for that audience is harder than some people like to admit. "Simple" is not the same thing as dull. Kids have an incredibly efficient boredom detector. If your design is flat, they will tell you, either with words or with their feet.

Then there is the **2026 In-Hand Game Design Contest**, where the goal is to create a game that does not require a surface, pockets, or anything beyond your hands. Voting ends **March 31, 2026**.

That is one of the more interesting prompts in the current batch, because it forces you to think about physical handling, information flow, and constraint in a very direct way. If you enjoy compact systems and tactile design problems, that one should be making a lot of noise in your head.

## Bottom line

There is enough variety in the current BGG contest lineup that most of us have lost the right to say, "none of these are really my thing." If you like small-box constraints, the 9-card contest is right there. If you think in direct interaction, the two-player contest is right there. If you like weird structural hooks, the deck-hand contest is right there. If you have a strong candidate for solo adaptation, the solomode contest is right there.

So pick one and get moving.

A finished contest entry is worth more than a brilliant concept that never leaves your notes app. And if nothing else, a good contest deadline has a way of forcing a design to stop being theoretical and become a game.
