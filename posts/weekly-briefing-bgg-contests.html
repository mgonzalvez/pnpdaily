Weekly Briefing: The Current BGG PnP Contest Run Is Stacked

By Martin Gonzalvez | March 11, 2026

If you have been thinking about entering a BoardGameGeek contest, this is a good time to stop circling and actually pick one. The current lineup is crowded in the right way: there are hard format constraints, audience-specific briefs, and one contest that is basically permission to finally make a proper solo mode instead of talking about it.

What I like about this batch is that they are not all asking for the same designer brain. You can go tiny, go family-friendly, go strictly two-player, go solo, or go weird and tactile. That matters, because most of us do not have one magical "contest game" sitting on a hard drive waiting to be renamed and uploaded.

The one most people should probably look at first

The 2026 9-Card Nanogame Print and Play Design Contest is exactly what it sounds like: one sheet of nine poker-size cards, rules pages, and up to 24 generic components if you want them. It is a clean constraint, which usually means it is a good design exercise even if you do not end up submitting. It runs through May 31, 2026.

This is the kind of contest that forces you to stop hiding behind "I just need a little more content." Nine cards is nine cards. You either have a game or you do not.

If your brain goes straight to head-to-head play

The 2026 Two-Player Print and Play Game Design Contest is focused where it should be: making something specifically for two players instead of pretending a multiplayer design also works at two. The brief is simple and correct: make an inexpensive, innovative two-player game. It also ends May 31, 2026.

That one is attractive because it is narrow without being tiny. There is still room for real interaction, tempo, bluffing, and positional pressure, but the contest itself is not asking you to solve for every player count under the sun.

If you want a strong hook out of the gate

The 2026 Deck Hand Games Design Contest asks for an 18-card game that works with a standard deck of playing cards. That is already a pitch. If your design brain likes starting from a component hook and then finding the game inside it, this is probably the cleanest prompt of the bunch. Deadline is June 26, 2026.

The danger here is obvious: do not let the standard deck become decorative. If the deck can be removed and the game still works, the hook was not doing enough.

If you have been side-eyeing a game that deserves a solo mode

The 2026 Solomode Contest is for designing a solo mode for a game that either has none or has one you think is not pulling its weight. Deadline is June 30, 2026.

This one is strong because it gives you permission to focus on development instead of pretending every worthwhile design task has to start from zero. There is a lot of bad solitaire grafting out there. A contest built around doing it properly is useful.

Two contests with very different constraints

The 2026 Children & Family Game Design Contest is aimed at games families can play with children nine and under, or games children can play together. Voting closes May 15, 2026. Designing for that audience is harder than people like to admit. "Simple" is not the same thing as flat, and kids will punish boring systems faster than adults will.

The 2026 In-Hand Game Design Contest is one of the more interesting constraints in the current lineup. The aim is to create a game that does not require a surface, pockets, or anything beyond your hands. Voting ends March 31, 2026. If you like compact, tactile systems and physical information handling, this is the one that should already be on your radar.

The practical takeaway

There is no shortage of excuses to sit out a contest cycle. Not enough time, wrong format, wrong audience, wrong component set, not polished enough. This current BGG lineup cuts through most of that. There is enough range here that you can almost certainly find one contest that fits the way you already design.

Pick the one that matches your natural instincts, not the one that sounds most prestigious in your head. If you are a compact-systems person, do the nine-card contest. If you think in interaction, do the two-player one. If you like structural constraints, do the deck-hand contest. If you have a strong candidate for solo adaptation, stop dragging your feet and enter the solomode contest.

The main thing is to enter something. A finished contest entry is worth more than a brilliant concept that never leaves your notes app.
